History + Fanon

How colonial violence evolved into mass incarceration

Frantz Fanon was a psychiatrist, revolutionary, and author whose work exposed how colonial powers maintained control over native populations not only through military force, but through psychological domination and social control. In his book The Wretched of the Earth, Fanon argued that systems of violence and oppression do not end with decolonization, they adapt and continue through institutions that appear “neutral.”

"The colonized can see right away if decolonization is taking place or not... The last shall be first and the first last."
– Frantz Fanon, The Wretched of the Earth

Today, the U.S. criminal justice system mirrors many of the colonial mechanisms Fanon described. Through hyper-policing, surveillance, and mass incarceration of Black and Brown communities, modern institutions replicate colonial patterns of control and racial subjugation. Prisons have become extensions of colonial infrastructure, reinforcing who is allowed to move freely and who is punished for merely surviving.

"In the colonies it is the policeman and the soldier who are the official, instituted go-betweens, the spokesmen of the settler and his rule of oppression."
– Frantz Fanon

This quote echoes through American history — from slave patrols to modern policing. The role of law enforcement has long served as a buffer between power and the people. The militarization of police, disproportionate incarceration of marginalized groups, and use of force in public protests all echo Fanon’s analysis of how violence sustains control.

"Violence is man re-creating himself."
– Frantz Fanon

While Fanon acknowledged the destructive nature of violence, he also saw it as a way for the oppressed to reclaim identity and agency when all other options are denied. In today’s context, this idea can be reinterpreted through the lens of protest, resistance, and abolitionist movements (acts not rooted in chaos), but in an urgent call for transformation. These movements aim not to destroy society, but to rebuild it around justice and dignity.

Fanon challenges us to examine how systemic harm is disguised as order. He forces us to ask: who benefits from punishment? Whose suffering is ignored? And what would it mean to truly decolonize justice?
