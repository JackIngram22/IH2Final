Profit + Arendt

What is freedom when justice is outsourced?

In What is Freedom?, Hannah Arendt argues that freedom is not simply about the absence of constraint — it’s about the presence of action. Freedom is not something we possess; it’s something we do, and it is inherently political. Arendt’s warnings about depoliticization and passive citizenship take on new relevance when applied to systems of mass incarceration today.

"To be free and to act are the same."
– Hannah Arendt, What is Freedom?

If freedom is rooted in action, then incarceration is not just confinement, it is the total stripping of freedom. Incarcerated people are denied participation in the world. Their actions, voices, and political agency are erased. Even after release, that erasure continues through disenfranchisement, surveillance, and social exclusion. Arendt’s definition of freedom helps us understand why mass incarceration is not just a legal or social issue, it is a crisis of democracy itself.

"Freedom, moreover, is not the same as liberation."
– Hannah Arendt, What is Freedom?

Arendt distinguishes liberation (being released from external control) from true freedom (the capacity to shape your world). The American legal system often treats release from prison as the final step — as if justice is complete once someone is "free." But as formerly incarcerated people know, that liberation does not come with restored rights, opportunities, or dignity. Freedom remains withheld long after the sentence ends.

"The trouble with modern liberties is that they concern only the liberties of the mind and not those of the person."
– Hannah Arendt, What is Freedom?

This observation hits hard in the context of the carceral state. People may technically have free speech or religious freedom, but what good are those liberties when you’re confined in a cell, denied autonomy over your body, and excluded from civic life? Arendt reminds us that true liberty requires not just rights on paper, but the ability to act and appear in public as a person among equals.

"Power springs up whenever people get together and act in concert."
– Hannah Arendt, What is Freedom?

Arendt’s emphasis on collective power challenges us to rethink incarceration not just as an individual punishment, but as a method of dismantling community power. Prisons isolate. They silence. They prevent people from organizing or participating in public life. In a privatized prison system, where profit depends on isolation, freedom becomes incompatible with the business model itself.

Arendt teaches us that freedom is not something granted by the state. It is something we enact together. That vision of freedom cannot survive a system that profits from confinement, silences political participation, and treats punishment as commerce. To move toward justice, we must reassert that freedom means action — not just absence of chains.
