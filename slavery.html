Modern Slavery

How the 13th Amendment left slavery alive in U.S. prisons

The 13th Amendment, ratified in 1865, abolished slavery in the United States — but with one critical exception: “except as a punishment for crime.” This clause created a legal pathway for forced labor to continue inside the criminal justice system, especially in the South. After emancipation, many Southern states passed Black Codes and vagrancy laws to arrest newly freed Black citizens and force them into convict leasing systems.

Convict leasing became a new form of slavery. Prisoners, mostly Black men, were leased out to private companies and plantations to perform backbreaking labor in brutal conditions. These systems generated revenue for local governments and corporations while costing human lives. In some states, the death rate for leased convicts exceeded that of enslaved workers before the Civil War.

Today, the legacy continues. Over 800,000 people are forced to work while incarcerated in the United States. In many states, prisoners are paid as little as $0.23 per hour, or not at all. They manufacture license plates, farm state property, clean public buildings, and even fight wildfires. Refusing to work can result in punishment, including solitary confinement or loss of visitation rights.

Under federal law, this labor is legal. The Supreme Court has upheld that incarcerated people are not protected by the same labor standards that apply to free citizens. In fact, the Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 does not cover people in prison. This means they can be paid far below minimum wage, have no right to unionize, and no protections against abuse or unsafe conditions.

Private companies and government agencies benefit from this system. Prison labor supports supply chains for major corporations and provides public services at low cost. While often framed as “rehabilitation” or “job training,” the reality is that many incarcerated people are coerced into labor without any meaningful path to employment after release.

It is not accidental that this system disproportionately impacts Black Americans. Black people make up about 13% of the U.S. population but nearly 38% of the prison population. The racial disparities in sentencing, policing, and imprisonment mean that the same communities once targeted by slavery and segregation are still exploited under the modern prison system.

The struggle against mass incarceration is inseparable from the fight against racialized economic exploitation. As long as the 13th Amendment loophole remains law, and as long as labor inside prisons continues to generate profit, the shadow of slavery will persist, not just as history, but as policy.
